What accounts for a country price spread?

The map shows physical CNECs and country allocation caps: the terms in our reconciliation of observed within-Core country price differences. It excludes virtual interfaces, whose cable-bound terms belong to a different decomposition.

This is accounting for a cleared result. It is not a calculation of how prices would change if a constraint were removed.

1. One grid, several kinds of variable.

Belgium and Germany each contain a meshed AC network. ALEGrO connects particular converter locations within those networks through a controllable DC link. Belgium is not a single physical bus wired to a virtual hub.

Connectivity schematic, not a substation map. Grey represents the surrounding AC network; green represents ALEGrO.

Capacity calculation compresses the grid’s response into a PTDF matrix. A country column represents a zonal injection pattern. A virtual-hub column represents a separately modeled interface exchange. Their coefficients differ because changing an injection’s location changes which lines it loads.

ALEGrO’s two endpoint variables are ALBE and ALDE. Other virtual hubs can represent AC borders or combined interfaces; “virtual” identifies a modeling device, not a type of cable.

Where the equations connect.

The same variables appear in the cable bound, the physical rows and the country balances. There is no separate cable solution attached afterwards.

vALBE+vALDE=0NBE=nBE+vALBENDE=nDE+vALDE

N is total country net export, n the country-hub position excluding ALEGrO, and v the virtual-hub position; all are MW. These equations isolate ALEGrO and assume lossless endpoint linkage.

Increase Belgian cable imports by 1 MW while holding both countries’ total positions fixed: ALBE changes by −1, ALDE by +1, BE by +1 and DE by −1. The displayed Maasbracht row changes by 0.27621 + 0.01254 − 0.27377 − 0.20992 = −0.19494 MW. This uses more cable capacity while relieving that particular AC constraint. Other physical rows respond through their own coefficients.

2. Clear all of it together.

Before clearing, the operators supply PTDFs, margins, interface limits and accounting relationships. Market participants supply orders. EUPHEMIA chooses accepted quantities and exchanges subject to the restrictions. The full algorithm also handles discrete orders and pricing rules; it is more than one simple linear program.

A positive shadow price measures the marginal increase in auction surplus from relaxing its restriction, under the applicable publication convention. It values extra capacity; it is not the amount by which a country’s price changes. A restriction can be tight with a zero shadow price.

The physical shadow prices and cable-bound shadow prices belong to the same solved system. Restricting ALEGrO changes the feasible routing and dispatch, which can change which physical constraints bind and their shadow prices. There is no separate step that adds the cable dual into a physical dual.

3. Account for the observed spread.

For the validated Core representation with a positive flow-based scaling factor α, the contribution of physical row k to country B minus country A is:

Ck, B−A=−μkα(ak,B−ak,A)
  • μk (mu): published shadow price of physical row k, in the publication’s €/MWh convention.
  • ak,A and ak,B: its country PTDF coefficients.
  • α (alpha): the dimensionless flow-based scaling factor, 0.9363136414 in this example. Core combines flow-based capacity with long-term allocation (LTA) capacity; α = 1 means flow-based only, α = 0 means LTA only. This accounting normalizes the published flow-based duals by α and requires α > 0.
  • Ck, B−A: the resulting contribution to the spread, in €/MWh.

Sum these physical terms and applicable country-cap corrections to reconcile the country spread. The physical shadow prices used here were determined with the cable restrictions enforced. A complete sum therefore does not imply that cable restrictions had no economic effect.

At the cleared optimum, the marginal value of changing the cable exchange balances the shadow value of its binding limit. This condition, called endpoint stationarity, equates the endpoint-component difference to the normalized cable-bound dual in this interval: 4.334550 / α ≈ 4.629378 €/MWh. The outer terms use the same physical duals with different PTDF columns. They are accounting differences, not prices of imaginary wires between a country and a hub.

The cable term replaces the endpoint difference inside the second decomposition; it is not an extra term in the first. Adding it to the map’s €2.601211 sum would give €7.230589/MWh, which is not the observed spread. The map therefore retains physical CNECs and country caps, without virtual-interface selectors or cable contribution rays.

To ask instead “how would each country’s price change with one more MW of cable capacity?”, we would need sensitivity analysis of the full clearing model or another clearing run. A published shadow price alone gives the marginal surplus value, not the distribution of country-price changes.

Scope and limits

This map covers Core bidding zones, not every European zone. The illustrated normalization requires α > 0; missing inputs leave attribution unavailable. Country allocation caps can create additional price separation. External interfaces can separate prices between regions even when one region has no internal congestion. The omission of virtual interfaces is a product-scope decision, not a claim that they never affect prices.

The observed fixture audit accepts residuals up to €0.02/MWh to accommodate publication precision. That tolerance is not an estimated omitted cable contribution.
