# Core allocation-constraint safeguards

**Source:** ACER Decision 10/2026, consolidated Core day-ahead capacity calculation methodology, 16 July 2026, [Annex II, official TSO copy](https://www.sepsas.sk/engine/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ACER-Decision-10-2026-on-Fourth-amendment-of-the-Core-DA-CCM-Annex-II.pdf), Articles 7 and 27; Annex 1. Checked 2026-11-12.

Article 7(3) requires published underlying analysis. If an [allocation constraint](../core-day-ahead-capacity-calculation.md#allocation-constraint) has a non-zero shadow price in more than 0.1% of a quarter’s hours, its report must assess each affected interval’s economic-surplus loss, security effectiveness and alternatives. Applicable, more efficient alternatives must be implemented. Net-position caps are transitional until 8 June 2028; an amendment request by 8 December 2027 can extend the period pending a decision. Article 27 gives regulators compliance data and complete reports where public versions withhold sensitive material.

Annex 1 calculates Poland’s export bound from available generation less unavailable capacity, demand and upward reserves. It explicitly contemplates zero exports. This establishes the mechanism, not the accuracy of inputs for an individual interval.

See [country-cap analysis](../country-import-export-caps.md#safeguards) for the policy debate; the [proposal’s explanatory document](core-da-ccm-fourth-amendment-explanatory-document.md) is a separate source.

### Transparency

Rechecked the consolidated methodology on 2026-11-16. Article 7(3)(a) requires publication of underlying analysis results, with calculation at least quarterly; Poland's Annex 1 specifies morning D-1 calculation separately for each MTU. Article 7(3)(b)'s trigger is non-zero shadow price in over 0.1% of a quarter's hours, not a numerical cap of zero. Once triggered, the report must analyse economic-surplus loss and security effectiveness for **each** MTU with non-zero shadow price, plus alternatives. Article 7(3)(c) requires applicable, more efficient alternatives to be implemented.

Article 25(2)(d)(viii) requires publication by 10:30 D-1 of each allocation constraint's Fmax and FLTN breakdown; Article 25(2)(e) adds shadow prices by 14:00. These are not an explicit requirement to publish every underlying Polish reserve, generation-unavailability and forecast input alongside each cap. Article 25(7) requires underlying capacity-calculation and market-coupling data related to quarterly reports to be supplied monthly to Core regulators. Article 27(1) requires compliance-monitoring data; Articles 25(6) and 27(3) allow additional publication/information requests, but a TSO may decline additional requests not made by its own competent regulator. URE is therefore a relevant route for demanding more PSE detail.

Articles 27(5)(a), 27(6) and 27(7) require the allocation-constraint assessments to be annexed to published quarterly reports, due within six weeks of quarter end. Public reports may omit commercial or critical-infrastructure-sensitive information, but regulators must receive complete versions. Aggregate public slides alone therefore cannot establish compliance or noncompliance with the per-MTU analysis obligation: the underlying and complete reports matter. The provisions require retrospective analysis, not case-by-case advance regulatory permission for each cap.

### Beyond-procurement

Annex 1, printed pp. 53–56, gives a broader rationale than morning procurement scarcity: residual demand left by market participants, forecast uncertainty, unit ramping and other plant constraints. The export formula subtracts generation unavailable through grid constraints (`PNA`) and exceptional expected unavailability (`PER`, for example cooling limitations or extended overhauls). Page 54 says balancing activation is “often exceeding the procured balancing capacity.” These are documented mechanisms and claims, not a demonstrated cause of each fully procured, binding interval in the investigation. Printed pp. 54–55 also attribute remaining activation needs to incomplete portfolio balancing and describe reserve procurement as illiquid and immature. These are the methodology’s stated explanations, not independent measurements of their respective contributions. Rechecked 2026-11-16; see the [public discussion](../country-import-export-caps.md#discussion).

The [December 2025 explanation](core-da-ccm-fourth-amendment-explanatory-document.md) says purchased capacity should leave day-ahead offers. Consequently, a fully procured interval requires an explanation of what additional limitation remained and whether the cap calculation incorporated those purchases. Neither document supplies the original per-interval inputs needed to resolve that question. Checked 2026-11-14.

### Oversight

The [16 July 2026 decision itself](https://www.acer.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/Individual%20Decisions/ACER-Decision-10-2026-Core-DayAhead-CCM-Amendment.pdf), checked 2026-11-16, establishes the extension's procedural record. Recitals 6 and 23 describe a joint Core-TSO amendment submitted to national regulators on 21 November 2025, with methodology, explanatory document and consultation report; it was not a standalone PSE application directly to ACER. Recitals 24(c), 28 and 37–40 show national regulators agreed on the Polish extension; referral to ACER arose from disagreement about long-term allocation inclusion. The extension changes four years after June 2022 implementation to six, ending 8 June 2028.

Recital 59 states ACER's agreement with the Polish extension without a numerical assessment in that paragraph. Recitals 60–63 discuss dates and terminology, not a quantified reserve or alternatives test. Recital 13 records additional written explanations on allocation constraints requested by ACER on 5 June 2026; their full contents were not retrieved in this review. Thus public evidence includes [procurement-shortfall charts](core-da-ccm-fourth-amendment-explanatory-document.md#figures), but neither a claim that regulators saw no figures nor a claim of independent numerical validation follows from the decision. The earlier statement below about no independently reproduced test remains limited to reviewed public material.

The [January 2025 Central Europe consultation report, response 12, printed p. 9](https://eepublicdownloads.entsoe.eu/clean-documents/nc-tasks/20250117%20CE%20DA%20CCM%20-%20Public%20Consultation%20Report.pdf) records a challenge to PSE’s evidence and analysis of alternatives. The TSOs respond that the reports are discussed at the Core Implementation Group, comprising TSOs, national regulators and ACER, and say there were no further objections. This is the TSOs’ account of review, not a published independent replication.

[URE’s December 2025 assessment](https://www.ure.gov.pl/download/9/15866/Ocenawplywureformyrynkubilansujacego.pdf), printed pp. 41–53, reviews activation using PSE data, assesses balancing reform and recommends additional measures. It documents notices to 823 generating-module owners in July–September 2025 over missing schedules, and requests stronger penalty powers. This demonstrates action on a contributor to reserve uncertainty, not verification of every individual cap. Recommendations include broader reserve access, qualification changes, scheduling and balancing incentives.

As checked on 2026-11-12, the public sources reviewed establish reporting, institutional discussion and regulator recommendations. They do not establish an independently reproduced per-interval necessity test or completion of every more efficient alternative. The [Q4 2025 assessment](pse-allocation-constraints-q4-2025.md) is evidence of an actual counterfactual calculation, with narrower scope than comparison against an improved market design.

### 2025-reports

The obligation predates the 2026 extension: the [third-amendment consolidated methodology published by CRE](https://www.cre.fr/fileadmin/Documents/Deliberations/2024/240926_2024-170_calcul_capacite_region_Core_Annexe2-methodologie.pdf), printed p. 18, already contains Article 7(3)(b)'s quarterly 0.1% trigger, per-MTU effectiveness/economic-surplus assessment and alternatives requirement, plus implementation under 7(3)(c).

All four 2025 quarterly Annex I introductions explicitly invoke the trigger. On 2026-11-16, inspection of each archive's allocation-constraint spreadsheet confirmed the following PSE rows: all have non-zero shadow prices and unique timestamps.

| Quarter / archive | Non-zero-price intervals | Spreadsheet share |
|---|---:|---:|
| [Q1](https://www.jao.eu/sites/default/files/2025-06/Core%20DA_CC_Q1_2025_report.zip) | 346 hourly | 16.03% |
| [Q2](https://www.jao.eu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Core%20DACC_Q2_2025report.zip) | 676 hourly | 30.95% |
| [Q3](https://www.jao.eu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Core%20DACC_Q3_2025report.zip) | 669 hourly | 30.30% |
| [Q4](https://www.jao.eu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Core%20DA_CC_Q4_2025_report.zip) | 2,736 quarter-hourly | 30.96% |

Q3's PSE slide 11 instead states 30.45%, with export constraints accounting for three hours (0.14%); the spreadsheet/deck discrepancy is unresolved. Even three export hours exceed 0.1% of the full 2,208-hour quarter. The Q3 simulation omits seven days for unavailable Simulation Facility data (slide 2). These qualifications do not change the threshold conclusion.

The PSE decks compare historical market clearing with clearing after removing Polish caps, keeping other market inputs unchanged. They then compare reserve availability with 1,000 MW upward / 500 MW downward thresholds and price demand/renewable curtailment. Q1 slide 7 predicts 22.9 GWh demand curtailment over 43 hours and 24.2 GWh renewable curtailment over 63 hours; Q2 slide 7 predicts 0.1 GWh demand and 160.6 GWh renewable curtailment. Q3 slide 9 predicts no demand curtailment and 163.3 GWh renewable curtailment over 238 hours. Q4 is assessed [separately](pse-allocation-constraints-q4-2025.md). These are modelled consequences, not observed curtailment attributable to cap removal.

The decks assess demand and renewable curtailment as remedial alternatives. They do not provide a comparative optimisation of additional reserve procurement, different plant commitment, storage/flexible demand or energy–reserve co-optimisation. Thus “no alternatives assessed” is too strong, but the public comparison is narrow. The companion spreadsheets provide timestamps, TSO and shadow prices, not interval-level reserve inputs or alternative-solution costs.

Q3 slide 10, visually checked, contains an internal inconsistency: removal produces a displayed Core surplus gain of €10.36m (Poland €14.02m), against €7.01m renewable compensation at €43/MWh, yet its prose says remedial costs exceed any surplus gain. Rechecking slides 6, 9–11 confirms that €10.36m includes Poland: slide 6 labels the change NoAC−AC and totals Poland’s +€14.02m with the rest of Core’s −€3.66m. Slide 9 reports zero demand curtailment; 163.3 GWh renewable curtailment at €43/MWh gives approximately €7.02m, consistent with the plotted €7.01m after rounding. Simple subtraction gives +€3.35m on those displayed components. The note on slide 10 incorrectly says Q4 2024; slide 6 establishes the −€3.66m refers to this Q3 2025 comparison. This does not establish a complete welfare result or operational feasibility; it does refute that slide's cost-comparison sentence on its own figures. Q2 slide 8 likewise shows €9.40m Core gain versus €4.74m renewable and €0.27m demand-curtailment costs while repeating the same cost-exceeds-gain sentence; that chart has been text-extracted but not visually verified. The public record does not establish whether regulators received additional alternatives analyses.
