How weighted interval score (WIS) works

READING WEIGHTED INTERVAL SCORE

Be precise. But pay for being wrong.

A very wide band is easy to land inside, but gives little useful precision. A very narrow band looks precise, but can miss badly. Weighted interval score (WIS) balances those costs and includes the median prediction’s error.

Illustrative numbers, not another experiment. In all three examples below, the predicted median is 100 and the observed price is 110 €/MWh. Only the bands change. Pale green is the 95% band, darker green the 50% band; the copper dot is the observed price. All diagrams use the same scale.

The calculation, in three costs

For the five quantiles used here, this is an exact rewrite of WIS, with the weights and normalization already combined:

1 · Median-error cost
0.2 × distance between the actual price and the median.
2 · Band-width cost
0.1 × width of the 50% band
+ 0.01 × width of the 95% band.
3 · Outside-band cost
0.4 × distance outside the 50% band
+ 0.4 × distance outside the 95% band.

Add the three costs to get WIS. Width means upper edge minus lower edge. Outside distance is zero when the actual price is inside a band; otherwise it is the distance to the nearest edge. A price outside both bands contributes to both miss terms.

These fixed weights come from the scoring definition for our five quantiles; they are not fitted to these results. All distances and score contributions are in €/MWh.

The median error is identical in all three cases. Wider bands add cost; when a price falls outside a band, the score adds a penalty that grows with the distance beyond its edge. WIS weights the two band scores and the median error together.

These are scores for one quarter-hour. The headline averages quarter-hour scores over all available outcomes in the selected period. Its units are €/MWh, but 16.34 is not the typical price error; the median’s MAE answers that question. Exact formula and weights ↗

How can the score improve while coverage is poor?

The reference’s bands are much wider. WIS combines that width cost with errors and misses; coverage asks only how often the outcome lands inside. Our system wins that combined comparison while its nominal 95% band contains only 63.7% of outcomes. The better score does not make those bands well calibrated. Both results belong in the headline.

What the numbers mean

WIS balances narrow bands against errors outside them. MAE / RMSE measure error in the median prediction. Coverage and width show whether uncertainty is useful and calibrated.

The reference uses yesterday’s and last week’s same-clock prices, plus training-only historical error quantiles. Its definition is available beside the comparison. Method ↗

Know which forecast you are reading

The picker says Forecast method. Its guide distinguishes three trained CatBoost models from a simple reference and automatic method selection. All are saved historical predictions.

Automatic selection (with fallbacks) chooses the demand model when inputs and execution permit, then falls back through weather, price history and the reference. Show the actual method used beside the daily result.

Names explain the inputs

Price-history model uses earlier same-clock prices, daily price summaries and calendar features.

Price-history + weather model adds forecast temperature, wind and radiation at twelve locations. Price-history + weather + demand model also adds German electricity-demand forecasts.

Yesterday / last-week reference averages those same-clock prices; when neither exists, it uses an earlier same-quarter median. Bands are calibrated from training-period errors. A failed trained model also uses reference predictions.

“Better score” is a finding.
It is not the whole conclusion.

One retrospective evaluation and uncertain historical input availability do not establish daily readiness, reliable extreme-price risk or trading profitability. The interface should make that understandable alongside the result.

03

Explain the computation.
Make the connections legible.

The default is a system-level view. The diagram explains the full method independently of any selected delivery day. Select a stage to see its inputs, transformation and outputs.

Arrows show required inputs. Dashed paths carry training examples; dotted paths carry scoring outcomes. The selection and feature code is reused across historical days. This is a teaching diagram, not a live execution monitor.

PRICE / STAGE EXPLANATION

Start with the market price

What comes in
Weekly quarter-hour JSON series from Bundesnetzagentur | SMARD.de, for the Germany–Luxembourg bidding zone. Values are in €/MWh.
What happens, and why
Parse delivery timestamps into UTC, check duplicates and retain negative prices. A price below zero is a market outcome, not a missing value.
What goes out
Earlier eligible prices supply lag features and training labels. Delivery-day outcomes are joined separately for evaluation.
Source contract ↗

WEATHER / STAGE EXPLANATION

Describe the weather the market could anticipate

What comes in
Open-Meteo Previous Runs, explicit German Weather Service ICON-EU model: hourly temperature at 2 m, wind at 120 m and shortwave radiation at twelve locations.
What happens, and why
Use the fixed previous_day2 forecast lead, documented as 48 hours. Keep temperature in °C, wind in km/h and radiation in W/m². These are forecast conditions, not realised weather.
What goes out
Geographical weather features provide information about weather-sensitive demand and renewable supply. They do not directly calculate generation.
Source contract ↗

DEMAND / STAGE EXPLANATION

Add the forecast level of electricity demand

What comes in
ENTSO-E monthly file extracts of Germany country day-ahead total load, in MW at quarter-hour resolution.
What happens, and why
Keep the Germany country series and its version timestamps. The input area is Germany; the target price area is Germany–Luxembourg. Preserve that distinction.
What goes out
A demand value for each eligible delivery quarter, or a missing value with an availability indicator.
Source contract ↗

LOAD / STAGE EXPLANATION

Make inputs stable enough to reproduce

What comes in
Archived source responses and retrieval metadata.
What happens, and why
Validate units, resolution, keys and conflicting rows. Store three typed Parquet tables with a fixed checksum manifest. Publish the manifest last, so an incomplete S3 snapshot cannot load.
What goes out
A versioned input snapshot. Local evaluation reads it without calling source providers again. Reproducibility alone does not establish historical availability.
Source contract ↗

SELECT / STAGE EXPLANATION

Separate future delivery from future knowledge

What comes in
Versioned prices, weather and demand, with delivery, source update and retrieval times kept distinct.
What happens, and why
For each forecast day, use the preceding day’s 11:00 Berlin deadline. Demand selects the latest nonmissing archived revision updated by that cutoff. Price and weather eligibility retain their stated timing assumptions. A forecast valid tomorrow can be eligible today.
What goes out
Only inputs eligible under the declared policy, with gaps and timing limitations visible. A later archived revision cannot reconstruct an earlier missing value.
Source contract ↗

FEATURES / STAGE EXPLANATION

Turn different clocks and meanings into model inputs

What comes in
Eligible price history, hourly forecast weather, quarter-hour demand and the local calendar.
What happens, and why
Price lags match the same local quarter 1, 2, 7 and 14 days earlier; daily summaries capture recent levels. Hourly weather is repeated across quarters. Radiation is shifted back an hour because its timestamp describes the preceding hour. Add calendar features and explicit missing-value flags.
What goes out
One feature row per delivery quarter. The three feature sets add weather and demand to the shared prices/calendar inputs. Future realised observations never fill gaps.
Source contract ↗

LABELS / STAGE EXPLANATION

Give training the right answers without leaking the future

What comes in
Realised prices for delivery dates before the monthly fit.
What happens, and why
Select label versions eligible at the fold’s first issue cutoff. Historical feature rows keep their original daily cutoffs; fitting later does not replace them with revised information.
What goes out
Past feature/price pairs for learning. These labels are distinct from the later outcomes used to score this fold.
Source contract ↗

TRAIN / STAGE EXPLANATION

Learn relationships; keep the comparison controlled

What comes in
The same eligible historical examples for three fixed CatBoost feature sets.
What happens, and why
Boosting combines decision trees to reduce quantile loss. Each percentile penalises errors asymmetrically. Expand training history at each month boundary, then reuse that month’s fitted models. Separately, estimate the reference’s error quantiles using training data only.
What goes out
Three fitted quantile models and a calibrated historical-price reference. The method and settings are fixed before the evaluation.
Source contract ↗

PREDICT / STAGE EXPLANATION

Convert a feature row into five prices

What comes in
Delivery-day features and the saved monthly models.
What happens, and why
Evaluate the fitted trees to obtain five quantile outputs. Sort their values into increasing order when raw outputs cross. The reference averages yesterday/last-week same-clock prices, with documented missing-history fallbacks, then adds training-error offsets.
What goes out
The 2.5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 97.5th percentiles for each quarter. These describe marginal uncertainty, not a joint daily price path.
Source contract ↗

FALLBACK / STAGE EXPLANATION

Keep the forecasting system defined when inputs fail

What comes in
Candidate model predictions, whole-source availability and execution status.
What happens, and why
Choose demand → weather → prices/calendar → reference as permitted by source availability and execution success. Partial gaps remain missing values. Never choose a model using the realised price or its score.
What goes out
A selected system forecast and an explicit fallback reason, both retained in the evaluation.
Source contract ↗

TRUTH / STAGE EXPLANATION

Bring in outcomes only after forecasting

What comes in
Realised SMARD auction prices for evaluated delivery quarters.
What happens, and why
Join by UTC delivery timestamp after generating predictions. This preserves both repeated autumn clock quarters. Leave absent truth missing and exclude those rows from every comparison together.
What goes out
Matched forecast/outcome rows for evaluation, without feeding outcomes back into historical prediction.
Source contract ↗

SCORE / STAGE EXPLANATION

Measure accuracy and uncertainty on the same evidence

What comes in
System, model and reference forecasts paired with the same available outcomes.
What happens, and why
Measure median errors, band width and observed coverage. Weighted interval score balances narrow bands against penalties outside them. Aggregate by day, month and full period; use seven-day blocks for uncertainty in the full-period score difference.
What goes out
Comparable results with sample counts and limitations. A lower score does not by itself establish reliable bands, live readiness or trading value.
Source contract ↗

Teach the transformation

“Build features” is not enough. Explain a same-clock price lag, how hourly weather aligns with quarter-hour prices, and why radiation timestamps shift. Explain what a fitted model learns and how predictions differ from training.

Evidence supports the explanation

Source versions, configuration, model artifacts and run provenance belong in expandable details. They let a reader verify the method. A per-quarter walkthrough is not required for this design.

05

What this review decides.

Agree the experience and explanatory depth first. The initial scope uses #105’s existing saved evaluation; it does not add model experiments, a scheduler or cloud deployment.

A reader can interpret the output

They can distinguish the median, quantile bands, realised price and reference, and understand what each metric says.

A reader can follow the method

They can explain where the inputs came from, how they were transformed and how training, prediction and scoring fit together.

A reader can judge the evidence

They can find the data period, source assumptions and limitations, and distinguish historical evaluation from live operation.

The views remain consistent

Names, definitions and facts agree across spec, results and pipeline. Provenance points to the exact evidence being shown.
